### A. Global Population Trends
World population growth accelerated after World War II when the population of less developed countries began to increase dramatically. A billion people were added to the world’s population between 1960 and 1975; another billion were also added between 1975 and 1987. The human population entered the twentieth century with 1.6 billion people and left the century with 6.1 billion.

Population growth and distribution differ significantly among the major regions. Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean all increased their share of the world population between 1970 and 2021. Asia’s share of the world population rose from 58% to 61%, Africa from 10 to 13%, the Caribbean and Latin America from 8 to 9%. During the same period (1970 and 2021), the share of Northern America declined from 6% in 1950 to 5% in 1998, and Europe from 18% to 12% (Bureau of Census, 2000). According to Population Reference Bureau (2021), by the mid of 2020, the world has more than 7 billion people. The continent of Asia is the most populous comprising 59.4% of the world population followed by Africa (17.5%). Although Asia as a continent has a majority of the world’s total population in 2020, estimated at 4.6 billion, the continent of Africa has contributed substantially to the rapid increase in the world’s population during this time. On the other hand, the total number of inhabitants of Oceania was only 43 million making the continent the least populated.

Our world has seen enormous changes in fertility rates and life expectancy over the past seven decades. For instance, in the early 1970s women had on average 4.5 children each; by 2015, total fertility for the world had fallen to below 2.5 children per woman. Meanwhile, average global lifespans have risen, from 64.6 years in the early 1990s to 70.8 years in 2020. However, although fertility levels have declined, they have not fallen at the same pace as mortality levels. Therefore, the world’s population showed continued growth.

**Why is the world population growing so rapidly?**
The high growth in the world population over the past centuries is largely the result of advances in modern medicines and improvements in living standards. These have significantly reduced infant, child and maternal mortality and have contributed to an increase in life expectancy of people. This in turn increases the world population.

#### II. Population Growth
Africa has one of the world’s fastest-growing population. The continent has been experiencing rapid changes in its population size as a result of many factors including the interactions between fertility and mortality. Despite the gradual decline in the birth and death rates globally, the continent continues to experience a high rate of population growth.

**Population doubling time** is the time it takes for a population to double in size or value. This can be computed using the **Rule of 70**. It involves dividing 70 by the growth rate (r) of the population at a specific time. Therefore;

$$dt = \frac{70}{r}$$

where; **dt** is doubling time, **r** is growth rate of the population.

**Example:**
If the population size of Ethiopia is 110 million in 2020 with a population growth rate of 2.5, how many years would it take for the population of Ethiopia to double itself?

$$dt = \frac{70}{2.5} = 28 \text{ years}$$

Therefore, it takes Ethiopia 28 years to double its population size to 220 million.

### A. Factors Responsible for Africa’s Rapid Population Growth
The interrelated factors are:
- high crude birth rates;
- high fertility rates;
- child birth at a young age;
- low rates of contraceptive use in most African nations;
- decline in infant mortality rates;
- decline in maternal mortality rates;
- decline in overall death rates; and
- increase in life expectancy; and decline in HIV/AIDS-related deaths.

### III. Population Change in Africa

#### Components of Population Change
Population change is a function of three variables: fertility (births), mortality (deaths) and migrations (immigration or emigration). There are four ways in which the number of people in an area can change:
- someone may be born in the area;
- an inhabitant may die;
- an outsider may move into the area; and
- a resident may move out.

These three “facts of life” are called **demographic variables**. The first two (Births and Deaths) are sometimes combined to form a composite variable: **natural increase**, or the excess (or deficit) of births over deaths.

#### The Balancing Equation
$$P_t - P_o = B - D + I - O, \text{ or } P_t - P_o = NI + NM$$

Where;
- $P_t$ = Population at the end of the period
- $P_o$ = Population at the beginning of the period
- $B$ = Births
- $D$ = Deaths
- $I$ = In-migration
- $O$ = Out-migration
- $NI$ = Natural Increase ($B-D$)
- $NM$ = Net Migration ($I-O$)

### I. Fertility Patterns
Fertility refers to the occurrence of birth in the human population.

**Definitions:**
- **Total Fertility Rate (TFR):** is the average number of children a woman would give birth to during her lifetime if she were to pass through her childbearing years (15-49 years) experiencing the present-day age-specific fertility rates.
- **Crude Birth Rate (CBR):** is the number of live births occurring among the population of a given geographical area in a given year, per 1,000 mid-year total population during the same year.

Mathematically,
$$CBR = \left( \frac{\text{Total annual number of live births}}{\text{Total mid-year population}} \right) \times 1000$$

**Example:**
Assume that a hypothetical African country ‘X’ had 2,407,500 live births in 2020 and it also had a total of 53,500,000 mid-year population in that year. Calculate the crude birth rate (CBR) for the country.

$$CBR = \frac{2,407,500}{53,500,000} \times 1000 = 45$$

Therefore, Country ‘X’ had 45 births per 1000 of the mid-year population in the year 2020.

### II. Mortality Patterns in Africa
Mortality refers to the occurrence of death in the human population.

**Definitions:**
- **Death Rate (DR):** is the number of deaths per 1000 population in a year.
- **Crude Death Rate (CDR):** is the number of deaths occurring among the population of a given geographical area during a given year, per 1,000 mid-year total population of the given geographical area during the same year.

Mathematically,
$$CDR = \left( \frac{\text{Total annual death}}{\text{Total mid-year population}} \right) \times 1000$$

- **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR):** is the death of an infant before his or her first birthday. It is measured as an annual number of infant deaths for every 1,000 live births during the same year.

Mathematically,
$$IMR = \left( \frac{\text{Total annual number of infant deaths}}{\text{Total annual live births}} \right) \times 1000$$

- **Maternal Mortality:** The annual number of female deaths from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management.
- **Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR):** is defined as the number of maternal deaths during a given time per 100,000 live births.

Mathematically,
$$MMR = \left( \frac{\text{Total deaths due to maternal causes}}{\text{Total annual live births}} \right) \times 100,000$$

### B. Sex Structure
The sex structure refers to the proportion of males to females in the overall population.

$$Sex Ratio = \left( \frac{\text{Number of male population}}{\text{Number of female population}} \right) \times 100$$

### C. Population Pyramid
A population pyramid is a graphical depiction of the age-sex composition of a population.

**Types of Population Pyramids:**
1. **Expansive pyramid:** Broad base, indicating high proportion of children and rapid growth.
2. **Constructive Pyramid:** Lower percentages of young people, large elderly population.
3. **Stationary Pyramid:** Unchanging pattern where births and deaths are balanced ($B = D$).

# UNIT FIVE: MAJOR ECONOMIC AND CULTURAL ACTIVITIES OF AFRICA

## INTRODUCTION
Africa is a very resourceful region, yet it faces huge economic problems. Its people are largely engaged in primary economic activity. Leaders have set goals called **Agenda 2063** to foster development and integration.

## 5.1 OVERVIEW OF EMPLOYMENT STRUCTURE IN THE WORLD

### KEY TERMS
- Developing country
- Economic activity
- Employment structure
- Lower middle-income
- Middle-income
- Upper middle-income

### Main Types of Economic Activities
- **Primary sector:** extracting raw materials, rearing animals, and growing crops.
- **Secondary sector:** changing raw materials into new products (manufacturing).
- **Tertiary sector:** service sector (education, health, tourism, banking).
- **Quaternary sector:** knowledge-based (ICT, research & development).

### Developing Countries (DVCs)
DVCs are the world’s least industrialized nations. They can be divided into:
- **Middle-income countries:**
    - Lower middle-income: GNI per capita between \$1,036 and \$4,045 (2021).
    - Upper middle-income: GNI per capita between \$4,046 and \$12,535 (2021).
- **Low-income countries:** GNI per capita of \$1,025 or less (2018).

## 5.2 MAJOR ECONOMIC ACTIVITIES IN AFRICA

### 5.2.1 Economic Activities in Africa

#### A. Primary Sector in Africa
- **i. Agriculture:** Provides employment for about two-thirds of the working population.
- **ii. Mining:** Major activity due to mineral wealth (copper, gold, diamonds).
- **iii. Forestry:** Africa has 624 million hectares under forests.
- **iv. Fishery:** Integral part of culture and economy.

#### B. Secondary Sector in Africa
Involves processing basic materials. Consists of textiles, clothing, footwear, and basic woods/metals.

#### C. Tertiary Sector in Africa
Involves selling services and skills. Tourism is growing in importance.

### 5.2.2 Major Factors Hindering Growth of Economy of Africa
1. **Limitations in Technology:** Lack of ICT and innovation.
2. **Over-dependence on Foreign Aids:** Leads to stagnation.
3. **Poor Structures of the Economy:** Exporting raw materials without adding value.
4. **Poor Education:** Need for graduates who can enhance the economy.

## 5.3 POSSIBLE SOLUTIONS TO THE PROBLEM OF UNEMPLOYMENT

### I. Main Features of Unemployment
- **Voluntary unemployment:** Jobless by choice.
- **Involuntary unemployment:** Willing and able to work but cannot find a job.

$$Unemployment Rate = \left( \frac{\text{the number of unemployed people}}{\text{The total number of people in the workforce}} \right) \times 100$$

### III. Solutions for Unemployment
1. The Creation of more Job Opportunities.
2. Individuals should be Encouraged to Enter Diverse Fields.
3. Sustainable Development (Society, Environment, Culture, and Economy).
